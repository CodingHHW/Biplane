A Biplanar Marker-Structure Framework for
Decoupled Error Assessment in 2D/3D Registration
for Intraoperative Planning and Navigation
Author Name

Abstract—Accurate intraoperative planning and navigation
require reliable spatial localization, but system-level error measurements often confound algorithmic, calibration, imaging,
tracking, and execution factors. This paper presents a biplanar
marker-structure framework for 2D/3D registration, target-point
recovery, and decoupled assessment of the core geometric computation. The method does not require preoperative 3D images,
digitally reconstructed radiographs, iterative optimization, or
GPU acceleration. Projection geometry is estimated from 2D–3D
marker correspondences, 3D target points are recovered from
multiview back-projection constraints, and the same calibrated
model forward-projects 3D points for 2D navigation display.
Experiments on two volume datasets under perspective and
orthographic projection settings yielded 810 valid observations.
The median target registration error was 0.150–0.205 mm, the
median reprojection error was 0.120–0.150 pixels, and the median
explicit computation-chain time was 0.659 s. View separation
was the dominant factor affecting 3D recovery accuracy and
repeatability, whereas the projection-model assumption had a
comparatively small effect. These results indicate that biplanar
marker geometry can provide an interpretable, reproducible
basis for algorithm-level evaluation before hardware-intensive
system integration. Source code is available at https://github.com/
CodingHHW/Biplane.
Index Terms—2D/3D registration, biplanar marker structure,
error decoupling, geometric calibration, intraoperative navigation, reprojection error.

I. I NTRODUCTION

MAGE-GUIDED orthopedic intervention and robotic assistance rely on accurate spatial relationships among intraoperative images, anatomical targets, instruments, and planned
trajectories. In many procedures, these relationships must be
inferred from limited two-dimensional (2D) X-ray or fluoroscopic views. Therefore, image-to-patient registration and geometric localization are central to the reliability, repeatability,
and safety of computer-assisted surgery [1]–[3].
A widely used solution is 2D/3D registration between preoperative computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance
(MR) images and intraoperative X-ray images. Intensitydriven methods typically synthesize digitally reconstructed
radiographs (DRRs) from preoperative volumes and optimize
an image-similarity objective [2], [5]–[10]. Multiview and
fluoroscopy-guided registration methods have demonstrated
the feasibility of this paradigm in orthopedic navigation

For intraoperative planning and navigation, two questions
remain insufficiently separated. First, when the final navigation
error is reported, it is often unclear how much of that error
arises from the core geometric algorithm rather than image formation, calibration, tracking, software implementation, instrument motion, or workflow execution. Second, for workflows
based on a known marker structure rather than patient-specific
CT-to-X-ray registration, the effects of view geometry, target
location, and projection-model assumptions are rarely analyzed as independent factors. Standardized 2D/3D registration
evaluations have emphasized controlled data, unified metrics,
and ground-truth geometry [27], [28], but most benchmarks
remain centered on CT-to-X-ray registration.
This paper investigates a different route: 2D/3D registration
and target recovery driven by a known biplanar marker structure. Here, “biplanar” refers to the spatial arrangement of the
marker structure rather than to biplanar X-ray acquisition. The
marker geometry supplies depth constraints that reduce the
degeneracy of point back-projection, while the same calibrated
projection model supports forward projection of 3D target or
instrument points for 2D navigation display. The objective
is not to replace system-level navigation validation, but to
quantify the algorithm-level accuracy, conditioning, and timing
of the core planning-navigation geometry under controlled
conditions before committing to hardware-intensive system
integration. Fig. 1 summarizes the assessment scope and a
staged validation strategy.
The contributions of this work are threefold:
• A biplanar marker-structure method is proposed for
2D/3D registration and target-point recovery without preoperative 3D images, DRR generation, iterative optimization, or GPU acceleration.
• A unified bidirectional geometry is formulated, in which
2D back-projection supports 3D target recovery and 3D
forward projection supports real-time 2D navigation display.
• A decoupled error assessment framework is established
using target registration error (TRE), reprojection er-

II. R ELATED W ORK
A. 2D/3D Registration for Image-Guided Intervention
Intensity-driven 2D/3D registration methods optimize a
similarity measure between intraoperative X-ray images and
DRRs generated from preoperative volumes. Mutual information, gradient-based measures, pattern-intensity formulations,
and multiresolution strategies have been used extensively [2],
[4]–[8]. These methods can achieve high accuracy but depend
on accurate projection geometry and may incur substantial
DRR computation cost [9]–[11]. In real intraoperative scenes,
content mismatch, tools, implants, tissue changes, and occlusion further challenge intensity-based objectives [12].
Feature-driven approaches reduce dependence on dense
intensity matching by using fiducials, anatomical landmarks,
instrument contours, or known components [29]–[31]. Such
methods are closely related to the present work because they
exploit prior geometry to regularize an otherwise ill-posed reconstruction problem. However, much of this literature focuses
on C-arm tracking, known-component localization, or quality
assurance rather than target-point recovery and algorithm-level
error decomposition for intraoperative planning and navigation.
Learning-based registration has expanded the design space
through convolutional pose regression, multi-agent refinement,
Riemannian pose-gradient estimation, iterative update operators, differentiable projective transformations, and differentiable X-ray rendering [19], [21], [24]–[26], [33], [34]. Realistic X-ray simulation and domain randomization have also been
used to improve transfer from synthetic to real fluoroscopic
images [22], [23]. These approaches address capture range
and computational speed, but they usually require training
data, simulation-to-real considerations, or patient-specific preoperative volumes. The method studied here instead targets
a lightweight geometric workflow in which a known marker
structure provides the primary spatial constraint.
B. Multiview Geometry and Evaluation
Multiview constraints are known to improve 2D/3D registration robustness [35]. Otake et al. combined fiducial-based
C-arm tracking, multiview registration, and GPU acceleration
for orthopedic image guidance [11]; related work has also used
3D–2D registration for vertebral level localization [36]. Biplanar X-ray constraints and statistical shape models have been
used for 3D bone reconstruction [37], and fluoroscopy-based
hip or pelvic navigation studies have used anatomical annotation, fragment pose estimation, and patch-based similarity
measures [13], [38], [39]. Recent work has revisited biplanar
X-ray-to-CT registration for navigation [40]. In contrast, the
present work uses a biplanar marker structure, not a biplanar
imaging setup, and focuses on geometric target recovery from
marker-constrained views.
Evaluation is equally important. A single final navigation
error is informative but does not reveal which subsystem

caused the error. Prior standardized evaluation studies and
verification methods have provided important foundations for
reproducible 2D/3D registration assessment [27], [28], [41].
Visualization and quality-assurance studies have also emphasized that how registration output is displayed can affect safety
and interpretation [42]. Motivated by these observations, this
work separately evaluates 3D recovery accuracy, 2D navigation consistency, view-geometry sensitivity, repeatability, and
computation time.

Given a known biplanar marker structure and its 2D observations in intraoperative views, the goal is to estimate the
projection geometry, recover the 3D target point P̂ from 2D
target observations, and forward-project recovered or updated
3D points to 2D views for navigation display. The resulting
geometric chain is evaluated independently from full-system
tracking and execution errors. This study treats marker and
target image coordinates as measured inputs; automatic marker
detection, segmentation robustness, and clinical image-quality
degradation are outside the present scope.
The workflow contains seven steps: constructing a geometric
simulation environment; acquiring multiview 2D projections of
the biplanar marker structure; calibrating projection geometry
from 2D–3D marker correspondences; back-projecting 2D
target points into 3D constraints; recovering a 3D target point
from multiview geometry; forward-projecting 3D target or
instrument points to 2D views; and computing decoupled error
metrics. Fig. 2 summarizes the workflow.
B. Simulation Environment and Marker Geometry
Experiments were conducted in a 3D Slicer-based geometric
simulation environment. The Slicer world coordinate system
represents the common reference frame; rendered volume data
represent the anatomical scene; a biplanar marker structure
represents the calibration fiducial; and an interactively updated
knife-point object represents the tracked instrument tip or
target point. Since C-arm calibration and fiducial localization
are important determinants of multiview registration accuracy
[11], [29], [32], the simulation abstracts these geometric components while excluding physical X-ray attenuation, scatter,
noise, soft-tissue appearance, and tool occlusion. Therefore,
the reported results characterize the algorithmic performance
of the geometric chain rather than the final accuracy of
a complete clinical navigation system. The correspondence
between the simulated components and a real intraoperative
navigation workflow is summarized in Fig. 3.

A. Experimental Setup
Experiments were performed on two volume datasets, denoted CTChest and Panoramix-cropped. Each dataset
was tested under perspective and orthographic projection settings. In each trial, three projection views were acquired.
The first two views were used for target-point recovery and
second-view RE computation; the third view characterized the
multiview acquisition geometry and view-separation condition.
The experiment was designed to test the geometric algorithm
under controlled inputs rather than to emulate the full physical
appearance of fluoroscopic images.
View separation was grouped according to the smallest
angle among the three views. For CTChest, the low, medium,
and high separation settings were 16.17◦ /0.40◦ /15.89◦ ,
46.99◦ /43.10◦ /67.87◦ , and 79.61◦ /73.60◦ /85.47◦ , respectively. For Panoramix-cropped, the corresponding settings were 17.00◦ /22.14◦ /14.70◦ , 35.41◦ /57.49◦ /56.59◦ , and
86.82◦ /81.34◦ /85.85◦ . Twenty target points were used in
each dataset. Overall, 810 valid observations were obtained:
387 from CTChest, 423 from Panoramix-cropped, 410
under perspective projection, and 400 under orthographic
projection.
Statistical analysis was performed at two levels. First,
single-trial errors were summarized and compared between
projection modes by independent-sample analysis. Second,
repeated trials were aggregated by dataset, target point, viewseparation group, and projection mode to analyze conditionlevel effects. View-separation effects were tested using the
Kruskal–Wallis test; distance effects were analyzed using
Spearman correlation; and repeatability was quantified by the
within-condition standard deviation of TRE and RE. Runtime
was summarized by the explicit computation-chain time, defined as the sum of marker initialization, dark-point extraction,
marker ordering, projection calibration, target-point update,
and TRE/RE computation. View acquisition, interface refresh,
and scene switching were excluded from this algorithmic
timing measure.

E. Sensitivity to Projection-Model Assumption
To further assess projection-model sensitivity, perspective and orthographic results were compared under matched
conditions with the same dataset, target point, and viewseparation group. A total of 59 matched condition units
were available, including 30 for CTChest and 29 for
Panoramix-cropped. Fig. 8 shows the distribution of
perspective-minus-orthographic differences.

Target location relative to the marker structure also influenced error. Fig. 9 plots condition-level error against the mean
target-to-marker distance. In CTChest, Spearman correlations were 0.5862 for TRE and 0.8249 for RE, both significant.
In Panoramix-cropped, RE also showed a strong positive
correlation with distance, whereas TRE was less consistently
explained by distance alone. These results suggest that average
distance is an important but incomplete descriptor; the full

is primarily governed by the conditioning of the multiview
constraints and the spatial distribution of targets relative to

standard deviation increased from 0.0513 mm in the highseparation group to 0.2275 mm in the low-separation group.
In Panoramix-cropped, it increased from 0.0508 mm to
0.2770 mm. By contrast, RE variability was smaller, with
median values approximately within 0.086–0.133 pixels.
V. D ISCUSSION
A. Methodological Interpretation
The proposed method should be interpreted as a markergeometry formulation rather than as a lightweight variant of
CT-to-X-ray registration. It does not estimate the pose of a
preoperative volume from intraoperative images, synthesize
DRRs, or optimize an intensity-based similarity objective.
Instead, it uses known biplanar marker geometry to convert
2D observations into spatial constraints and to recover target
points in a shared world coordinate system. This mechanism
is closer to fiducial- or known-component-driven methods
[29]–[31], but the endpoint is different: target-point recovery
and navigation-display projection for planning and navigation,
combined with algorithm-level error assessment.
The main empirical finding is that view geometry dominates
3D recovery accuracy. Low view separation reduced the independence of the spatial constraints and increased both TRE
and TRE variability. This behavior is expected from multiview

The results also show that target-to-marker distance is
associated with error but does not fully determine it. Some
targets at similar distances exhibited different error levels, and
some intermediate-distance targets produced relatively high
errors. This indicates that the specific geometric configuration,
not merely distance, affects the conditioning of the recovery
problem.
Image scale is another plausible factor. Repeated acquisitions may differ slightly in field of view and magnification,
changing the pixel footprint of both target and marker points.
Larger projected markers and targets generally support more
stable point localization, whereas smaller pixel coverage increases sensitivity to sampling and manual interaction. Therefore, the observed location-dependent errors likely reflect both
3D spatial geometry and 2D image-scale variation.
C. Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, the experiments
were conducted in a geometric simulation environment and
did not model X-ray attenuation, scatter, detector noise, tool
occlusion, implant artifacts, soft-tissue changes, or automatic
marker detection. The reported results should therefore be interpreted as algorithm-level geometric performance rather than
final clinical navigation accuracy. Second, RE was evaluated
on discrete static target points; continuous instrument motion, latency accumulation, and human-computer interaction
effects were not analyzed. Third, the marker structure and
target distribution were fixed for the present experiments, so
generalization to other marker designs, anatomical regions, and
imaging conditions requires further study. Finally, image scale
and pixel coverage were not explicitly controlled, although
the results suggest that they affect point localization and
downstream error.
These limitations should be interpreted in relation to the
intended validation stage. The aim of the present study is

tracking, and workflow errors, and are therefore reserved for
a later translational phase. At the present stage, the evidence
can be strengthened within the same low-cost framework by
adding controlled 2D marker/target perturbation tests, markerlayout and view-selection ablations, synthetic image-scale
analyses, and fully reproducible code and data logs.
VI. C ONCLUSION
This paper presented a biplanar marker-structure framework
for 2D/3D registration, target-point recovery, and decoupled
error assessment in intraoperative planning and navigation.
The method uses 2D images, projection calibration, and analytic geometry to recover 3D target points without preoperative 3D images, DRR generation, iterative optimization,
GPU acceleration, or dedicated surgical hardware. In two
simulated volume datasets, the method achieved submillimeter TRE, subpixel RE, and subsecond explicit computationchain time. View separation was the dominant factor affecting
3D recovery accuracy and repeatability, whereas projectionmodel choice had a smaller effect. These findings support
controlled geometric evaluation as a practical pre-hardware
stage for establishing algorithmic feasibility before systemlevel navigation integration.
